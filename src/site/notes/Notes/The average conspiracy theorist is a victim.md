# The average conspiracy theorist is a victim

Conspiracy theorists can roughly be put into three categories:

- People who don't believe but intentionally recruit (grifters)
- People who believe but don't intentionally recruit
- People who believe and intentionally recruit

I would argue, from personal experience, that most conspiracy theorists fall into the second category - they believe but do not intentionally recruit others. [[Intentionally malicious people do not deserve my empathy\|Intentionally malicious people do not deserve my empathy]], but the average conspiracy theorist isn't malicious. If anything, they are a victim of misinformation and, sometimes, community.

A conspiracy theorist often starts out as a normal member of society. Something happens that leads them towards conspiracy. Perhaps they developed a distrust for authority, were directed towards disinformation by social media algorithms, were radicalised by a friend or family member, were fooled by a grifter, or are simply seeking an answer to something that seems so disorienting and chaotic. Whatever the reason, the outcome is usually the same: Conspiracy theorists tend to develop a suspicion of authority, academia, and other establishments; they believe that things always happen for a reason (e.g. someone ‘pulling the strings’); they feel enlightened, or even intellectually superior; and they base their arguments on emotion. But we, especially those of us who are scientifically inclined, need to take a step back and think about the effect conspiracy theories have not only on the general public, but on conspiracy theorists themselves.

- If a conspiracy theorist has been indoctrinated by a grifter, they are being manipulated. Their belief is being used to swindle them out of money or to contribute to someone’s sense of self-importance.
- If a conspiracy theorist has been falsely arrested by the police, they may (understandably so) lose faith in the police force’s ability to protect them.
- If the YouTube algorithm has been steadily directing a conspiracy theorist towards increasingly radical content, they may not even realise there is an alternative set of beliefs.
- If a conspiracy theorist’s child has fallen gravely ill for an unknown reason, they may make connections of coincidences.

When a conspiracy theorist joins a community of like-minded people, their beliefs are reinforced without challenge. They feel a sense of belonging and support, as well as stability within their echo chamber. They find the answers the feel society could not provide them with. The answers society would judge them for.

The problem is that victimhood does not absolve harm. We can understand the circumstances that lead someone to conspiracy and radicalisation while still acknowledging that their belief is harmful. Even someone who doesn’t recruit others can reinforce dangerous misinformation. Or, more concerningly, they can act in accordance to their beliefs. Someone who doesn’t vaccinate their child is still exposing them and other children to unnecessary risk. Someone who believes trans people are grooming children may still commit a hate crime. [[Victim and abuser are not mutually exclusive\|Victim and abuser are not mutually exclusive]].

It’s hard to deradicalise someone. In some cases, it may be impossible. But I know that [[Escaping radicalisation requires social support\|escaping radicalisation requires social support]] and that treating people who are trying to better themselves, trying to escape, is counter-productive. We need to provide a supportive environment for these people. We need to replace the community they found in conspiracy. And more than anything else at all, we need foster critical thinking skills and education. The best way to reduce conspiracy theorists is to prevent them.
